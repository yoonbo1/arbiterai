# Where Arbiter goes next
**Research agenda, 8 September 2026.** Every claim below carries a source. Numbers that
came from a single source or that I could not verify are marked. Written after four
parallel research streams covering the AI job market, the production engineering stack,
the benchmark landscape, and what large-scale work actually costs.

---

## The short version

Stop thinking of Arbiter as a clinical document pipeline that happens to have an eval
harness. The harness is the asset. The market is paying top-of-band for exactly one
skill and it is not building AI systems, it is proving what they do and do not do.

Three weeks ago the FDA published a discussion paper proposing how generative AI medical
devices should be benchmarked, and asked the public to respond. It asks, in numbered
questions, the precise things Arbiter's published post-mortems already answer with data.
**Comments close 19 October 2026, 41 days from today.** That deadline sets the plan.

## 1. What the market actually wants

### The gap, quantified

LangChain surveyed 1,340 practitioners (published 2026-06-12, fielded Nov–Dec 2025):

| | share of teams |
|---|---|
| Have agents in production | 57.3% |
| Have implemented observability | 89% |
| Run offline evaluations | 52.4% |
| Run online or production evaluations | 37.3% |
| Run no evaluation at all | 29.5% |

Top barrier to production is **quality at 33%**, ahead of latency and cost. Teams can
see what their system did. They cannot judge whether it was right.

Corroboration from four independent directions. Menlo Ventures (2025-12-09, n=495): only
**16%** of enterprise deployments are true agents, 84% are workflows. McKinsey State of AI
2026: fewer than **10%** of organisations have agents deployed in any function. Databricks
telemetry across 20,000+ organisations (Jan 2026): **19%** deployed at scale. Buy-versus-
build flipped from 47/53 to **24/76 in a single year** (Menlo) because enterprises cannot
staff this work.

### Three claims circulating that are false

Worth knowing so they are never repeated in anything published under this name. The
"1.6M open AI roles versus 518K qualified candidates" figure traces to a staffing vendor's
marketing page with no study behind it. The "LinkedIn median AI Engineer $166K" number is
not in LinkedIn's Jobs on the Rise 2026 report, which contains no salary data. There is no
Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2026; posts citing one are relabelling 2025.

## 2. The opening, and its deadline

On **18 August 2026** the FDA's Center for Devices and Radiological Health published
*Considerations for the Regulation of Generative AI-Enabled Medical Devices: Discussion
Paper and Request for Feedback*, docket **FDA-2026-N-7874**. Comments close **19 October
2026**. It is explicitly not guidance and not policy. It is the agency asking how this
should work, before deciding.

It proposes a competency-based framework: device benchmarking, then clinical confirmation.
Benchmarking has ten elements.

| Group | Elements |
|---|---|
| Safety | S.1 safety-critical recognition and escalation · S.2 scope maintenance and boundary adherence · S.3 calibration, uncertainty communication, clinical deferral |
| Clinical proficiency | E.1 clinical knowledge and task fidelity · E.2 information gathering and clinical analysis · E.3 quantitative and measurement analysis · E.4 communication quality and user comprehension |
| Generalizability | R.1 robustness, reliability, reproducibility · R.2 subgroup performance |
| Agentic | A.1 competencies specific to agentic devices |

Postmarket, it proposes periodic re-benchmarking, sample-based clinician review, and
performance degradation monitoring.

### Why this docket matters more than anything else on the list

The numbered questions are answerable from work already published on arbiterai.tech.

**Question 10.** *"How should a sponsor establish that performance on a given benchmark
predicts safe and effective real-world behavior... What evidence should support the
construct validity of a benchmark used to gate device evaluation, and what role should
sponsor-developed benchmarks play given potential concerns around independence and
optimization to the test?"*

This is the post-mortem "Our de-id recall read 0.967 while ZIP codes survived in 14 of 20
documents." A sponsor-developed benchmark, measured by its own author, whose headline
number was valid and whose construct was not. Recall read 0.967 while by component it was
about 0.81, because the check compared whole 40-character address strings and only the
city was ever tokenized. That is a construct-validity failure with the numbers attached.

**Question 13.** *"What safeguards would mitigate the risk that synthetic data generated by
models of the same class as the device under evaluation reproduces the very performance
gaps the evaluation is intended to detect?"*

Arbiter's corpus is generated by templates and Faker, not by a language model, which is
the safeguard the question asks for. And the published caveat already states what the
synthetic number does not prove: Faker names are clean and capitalised, so 1.000 on
synthetic records says nothing about real names.

**Question 20.** *"Whether the proposed approaches to postmarket monitoring can be
facilitated by machine-based supervisory agents. What considerations, including the
evaluation and reliability of the supervisory agent itself, should CDRH take into
account?"*

This is the post-mortem "The 7B judge scored identical facts 0.0 or 1.0 depending on where
the citation sat." A machine supervisor, deterministic at temperature zero, that rejected
five of forty correct answers on formatting. It is a measured demonstration of why a
supervisory agent must itself be validated before it can gate anything.

**Question 12** asks how to combine synthetic and real evidence into a single performance
estimate. **Question 19** asks about cadence and triggering events for re-benchmarking.

A comment on a federal docket is free, permanent, publicly citable, and attributed. It is
the cheapest credential available in this market and it expires in 41 days.

### What the rest of the regulatory calendar says

**1 January 2027** is a deadline cluster, 115 days out: all four CMS-0057-F FHIR APIs
including Prior Authorization, Colorado SB 26-189 developer documentation, and four state
prior-authorisation AI laws.

The FDA replaced its Clinical Decision Support guidance on **6 January 2026**, loosening
it. Enforcement discretion now extends to software giving a single clinically appropriate
recommendation, and the transparency criterion relaxed to "a summary of the general
approach." Two firms independently noted the guidance never mentions generative AI or
language models at all. The August discussion paper is the answer to that silence.

Separately, ASTP proposed in December 2025 to **delete the AI model-card requirements**
from the health IT certification criteria and has not finalised nine months later. The
requirement is live but unowned, with no announced replacement. Nobody is filling the
transparency gap that leaves.

## 3. What Arbiter already has

Mapping the existing harness onto the FDA elements, honestly.

| FDA element | Arbiter today | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| S.1 escalation | Single escalation, skipped when tiers are identical | Escalation never changes a verdict on one model |
| S.2 boundary adherence | Row-level security, hard patient filter, cross-tenant probes, 0 leaks every run | Nothing measures refusal of out-of-scope questions |
| S.3 calibration and deferral | Validation gate returns no answer below faithfulness 0.7 | No calibration curve; the threshold is a guess |
| E.1 task fidelity | Answer accuracy 0.975 on 40 gold questions | 40 questions is far too few |
| E.2 information gathering | Hybrid retrieval, citations required | Not measured separately from answering |
| E.3 quantitative analysis | Lab and medication extraction, F1 1.00 / 1.00 / 0.99 | Synthetic only |
| E.4 communication quality | Not measured | Entirely absent |
| R.1 robustness and reproducibility | OCR corpus, deterministic at temperature 0 | No run-to-run variance measurement |
| R.2 subgroup performance | Not measured | Entirely absent, and this is the one regulators care most about |
| A.1 agentic | Not applicable, the pipeline is a fixed graph | — |

Six of ten elements have something behind them. The four gaps are the interesting work.

The other asset is the failure record. Four published post-mortems, each naming the bug,
the number, the fix, and the test. That is the artifact the market cannot fake: a repo
with an eval harness is common, a repo showing the harness was wrong and how the author
found out is not.

## 4. Where the benchmark gap is

Four research streams converged on the same conclusion from different directions: the
measurement layer is the weakest part of the field, and it is where these assets are
strongest. One gap is far better evidenced than the rest.

### There is no independent clinical de-identification benchmark, and the incumbent numbers have a conflict of interest

Every de-identification comparison in industry circulation comes from a vendor that is
also the top scorer. John Snow Labs has published at least eight comparison posts across
2025 and 2026 in which its own pipeline wins:

| System | Reported PHI F1 |
|---|---|
| John Snow Labs Healthcare NLP | 0.96 |
| Claude Opus 4.8 | 0.91 |
| GPT-5.5 | 0.89 |
| Gemini 3.1 Pro | 0.86 |
| Databricks `ai_mask()` | 0.71 |
| OpenAI Privacy Filter | 0.55 |

The peer-reviewed anchor for this series (arXiv 2503.20794, April 2025) has **all five
authors employed by John Snow Labs**, evaluates on 48 internally annotated notes, and
carries no conflict-of-interest statement. These figures are now cited across the industry
as though they were independent.

### The reference standards are themselves wrong

Palacios, Neeley et al. at Baylor and Texas Children's (arXiv 2608.17051, August 2026)
found that language models surfaced **414 identifiers absent from the gold annotation**.
All 49 sampled were confirmed genuine PHI by expert review. Re-annotating ten
high-discrepancy notes added **227 previously missed spans**. Prompts that named
institution-specific categories recovered 79% of the missed identifiers, and were
**penalised by the benchmark for doing so**, because the annotation did not contain them.

Stanford Medicine's SHIELD (arXiv 2605.03301) shows the same category is the least
transferable: universal types move across institutions cleanly, with dates at 0.94,
identifiers 0.96 and phone numbers 0.99, while **hospital names collapse to 0.02 recall**.

So institution-specific PHI is simultaneously the hardest category to detect, the least
transferable, the most under-annotated in every public benchmark, and squarely inside
HIPAA's catch-all for any other unique identifying number, characteristic or code.

### Over-redaction is unmeasured, and it harms patients

Aghakasiri et al. (arXiv 2509.14464) name the field's three failures: inconsistent metrics
that prevent comparison, classification metrics that miss damage to clinical content, and
no manual validation of automated metrics. They measure that **18% to 89% of false
positives cause clinically relevant changes**, with 70% to 80% rated high-severity by
physicians. Their content-preservation metric correlates with manual annotation at r=0.78
against r=−0.34 for the incumbent metric.

Even John Snow Labs now concedes the point. A post dated 2026-09-01 argues aggregate F1
is the wrong frame because there is no partial credit, and that compliance requires
surrogate consistency, deterministic tokenisation, date-shift interval preservation, and
span-level audit records. **Nobody scores any of those today.** Arbiter already produces
three of the four.

### What the surrounding market says

Stanford's MedHELM scores models by category. Patient communication 0.78–0.83. Clinical
note generation 0.73–0.85. Clinical decision support 0.56–0.72. **Administration
0.53–0.63, the weakest category.** Administration is where 2026 capital went: more than
$500M into AI revenue-cycle by August, Cohere Health at $90M for prior authorisation,
Tennr at $101M processing 10M documents a month. Healthcare is the largest vertical in
enterprise generative AI at $1.5B of $3.5B. Ambient scribing is saturated at 92% of health
systems.

And the obvious approach already failed. CHAI's national assurance-lab network collapsed
by early 2025; its own leader said the hypothesis that pre-procurement certification was
what clinicians wanted turned out to be wrong. The American Hospital Association asked HHS
on 2026-02-23 for post-deployment standards. CHAI has since published testing frameworks
for ambient and agentic AI with **no independent benchmark producing numbers against
them**.

### The statistical requirements, which are where most independent benchmarks die

Three findings set the bar, and meeting it is most of the credibility.

**Cluster the standard errors.** Miller (arXiv 2411.00640) shows that when items share
source documents or patients, effective sample size is a fraction of nominal, and
clustered errors on popular evaluations run more than three times naive ones. An
independent benchmark that reports naive errors will be dismissed on sight.

**Correct the judge, do not trust it.** Prediction-powered inference combines a small
human-labelled set with a large judge-labelled set and returns valid confidence intervals
with the judge's bias removed. GLIDE (arXiv 2605.31278) packages the methods with a
scipy-style API. Nobody has published the label-efficiency curve: how many human labels
buy what interval width at what judge quality. Every team doing this work needs that curve
and none of them has it.

**Measure the judge itself.** MedHELM's model jury reaches an agreement of 0.47 with
clinicians while clinicians agree with each other at 0.43. Norman et al. (arXiv 2606.19544,
541,000 judgments across 21 judges) found judge rankings shift by up to 14 positions across
benchmarks, and that two production judges combine above 0.95 test-retest reliability with
real position bias. Perfectly reproducible and perfectly wrong.

One honest tension worth holding. Xiao and Cheng (arXiv 2609.02899) tested 47 models and 74
deliberately contaminated variants and found rank correlation of 0.997 between standard and
contamination-controlled leaderboards. If that replicates, contamination resistance is a
weaker selling point than **validity**, which is measuring the right thing. That happens to
be where all of this work sits.

## 5. What large scale actually costs

Renting compute is cheap. The constraint is time, data, and an institutional email.

| | price |
|---|---|
| H100, cheapest credible dedicated | $2.20/hr |
| 8×H100 node | $21–24/hr |
| Full small-model training pipeline, end to end | $92 |
| Competitive 32B reasoning fine-tune | ~$450 |
| Batch API discount | 50% |
| Prompt caching discount | 10× on the cached prefix |

Batch plus caching stack. A sweep with a fixed system prompt costs $0.10 per million
tokens on Sonnet 5 against $2.00 undiscounted, a 20× reduction. Any benchmark run should
be built this way from the first line.

Self-hosting essentially never beats an API on cost for one person. To beat a hosted open
model you would need to generate roughly 13 billion output tokens a month at full
saturation. The only sound reasons to self-host are data that cannot leave the machine,
a custom fine-tune nobody hosts, or research access to logits. The first one is real here.

**The M4 with 32 GB is a development machine, not a training machine.** 120 GB/s of memory
bandwidth and about 21 GB addressable by default, 26 GB if the wired limit is raised. A
2021 M1 Max beats it at decode because bandwidth, not generation, is what matters. The
sweet spot is a mixture-of-experts model at 4-bit, around 18 GB and 25 tokens/second.
Fine-tuning a 7B locally means batch size 1 and 4 of 32 layers; the same job is 20 minutes
and about $2 on a rented H100.

**Free compute that is actually open to an unaffiliated individual:** TPU Research Cloud,
more than 1,000 Cloud TPUs, renewable 30 days, no stated affiliation requirement, on
condition that results are published. Modal, $30/month. Hugging Face Pro, $9/month for 25
minutes a day of an H200. Microsoft for Startups, up to $150K, explicitly open to
bootstrapped solo founders.

**Closed to a personal email address:** NAIRR states outright that submissions from Gmail
addresses will not be considered. NSF ACCESS, AWS Cloud Credit for Research, and Google's
research credits all require an institution. A guest or visiting-researcher affiliation at
any accredited institution converts that entire column, and costs one email.

## 6. The plan

### Horizon 1, now to 19 October: the docket

Write and file a substantive comment on FDA-2026-N-7874. Answer questions 10, 12, 13, 19
and 20 from measured experience, not opinion. Every assertion cites a number from a public
repository, and the repository is open for anyone to rerun.

The argument to make, in one line: a benchmark that gates a medical device must have its
construct validity demonstrated, not assumed, and the only evidence that counts is a
documented case where the benchmark was wrong and the author found out.

Before filing, close two gaps so the comment describes a harness that already does what it
recommends. Add run-to-run variance measurement, which is R.1 and currently missing. Add a
calibration curve for the faithfulness judge against human labels, which is S.3 and Q20,
and is the single most defensible thing in the whole submission.

Cost: nothing but time.

### Horizon 2, October to January: the benchmark

Build the independent clinical de-identification benchmark. It is the best-evidenced gap
found by any of the four research streams, the incumbent numbers have a documented conflict
of interest, and it is the outward-facing version of a harness that already exists.

What makes it defensible rather than just another leaderboard:

1. **Score per identifier type, never aggregate.** Aggregate F1 hides exactly the failure
   that matters, and even the incumbent vendor now says so.
2. **Score over-redaction alongside recall.** Content loss is clinically harmful, is
   currently unmeasured, and there is a validated metric for it.
3. **Score the compliance properties nobody scores.** Surrogate consistency, deterministic
   tokenisation, date-shift interval preservation, span-level audit records.
4. **Audit the gold standard as part of the protocol.** Run an ensemble, surface
   high-discrepancy spans, adjudicate a sample, publish a corrected recall estimate and an
   estimate of how wrong the benchmark was. That is a publishable result on its own.
5. **Include the artifact nobody tests.** Scanned faxes, referral forms, intake packets.
   The corpus generator already rasterises, tilts, blurs and thresholds pages.
6. **Clustered confidence intervals on every number**, prediction-powered correction for
   every judged metric, published cost and token counts, an open harness, a versioned
   dataset with a canary string, and a private held-out tier.
7. **Evaluate vendor pipelines and frontier models under one harness**, including the
   pipelines whose published numbers currently have no independent check.

Prerequisites in order: extend the generator beyond discharge summaries; get an external
SSD, since 62 GB free is not enough; add multi-provider support to the harness, which
already speaks the OpenAI wire format; and build the four missing FDA elements, of which
subgroup performance is the one regulators care most about.

Cost is low, roughly $1.3K to $3K for a 20-model batched sweep, because the documents are
short and nothing is agentic. Paired analysis on identical items is worth up to a 3.3x
effective cost reduction and is free. Batch is another 2x, caching up to 10x. Buy items,
not repeats: tripling the item count buys about 42% error reduction for the same money
that three repeats spend on 15%.

The real cost is expert adjudication time, and that is also the moat.

### Horizon 3, 2027: continuous

Re-benchmark on a cadence, publish drift, and let anyone rerun it. That is the
post-deployment monitoring CHAI failed to deliver, the AHA asked HHS for, and FDA proposed
in August. Being early and independent is the whole moat.

### In parallel, this week and free

- **Anthropic Fellows Program** is open now, three tracks, remote-friendly in the US. The
  fit is ML Systems and Reinforcement Learning. Stipend plus compute.
- **TPU Research Cloud.** Apply. It costs an email and the condition is publication, which
  is the plan anyway.
- **An institutional affiliation.** The highest-leverage administrative act available.

## 7. What I am not recommending, and why

**Inference and serving optimisation.** The best-paid narrow cluster in the market, named
identically by five companies. But it needs GPUs and a real production workload, and it
competes directly with well-funded teams who have both. The evidence would take a year to
assemble and would still be weaker than theirs.

**Agent frameworks.** The most-mentioned skill in postings, and the most crowded. The
differentiated position is not building agents, it is measuring them, which the plan
above already covers under A.1.

**Pretraining anything.** A frontier run is 1,000 to 10,000 times the available compute,
and more decisively it needs a team on call. Llama 3 took 419 unexpected interruptions in
54 days. That is not a solo activity.

**A run-to-run reliability leaderboard**, tempting as it is. Princeton's Holistic Agent
Leaderboard paused new submissions specifically to build this, which proves the gap is
real, but it needs $15K to $40K of agentic rollouts and the incumbent has released
infrastructure and a head start. Revisit it if NAIRR or Schmidt funding lands.

**Chasing a general leaderboard.** Saturated, contaminated, and dominated by organisations
with staff. The defensible position is a domain nobody has covered, measured properly,
by someone who has already published being wrong.
